# Short: "Lightning just hit the ocean. Why are the fish still alive?"

**Length:** ~36–38 s · **Format:** 9:16, 1080×1920 · **Rule:** no still images, every shot is a moving clip

## Why this topic

- Every shot is naturally full of motion: storm, lightning, waves, fish, light under water.
- The first frame is a hook on its own (lightning hitting the sea), so no title card is needed.
- The answer is surprising, but it's true and fits in 35 seconds.
- It ends with a twist (the danger is anything sticking out of the water), which loops back to the opening strike.

## Fact base (keep the script within this)

- Lightning carries roughly 100 million to 1 billion volts, so "hundreds of millions" is safe to say.
- Seawater is a strong conductor, so the current spreads outward across the surface instead of travelling downward.
- The effect is concentrated in the top few metres and falls off quickly with distance and depth.
- Seawater conducts electricity better than fish tissue, so most of the current goes around a fish rather than through it.
- Fish right at the surface, near the strike point, can still be killed. Don't claim that "fish never die".
- Swimmers and boats are at risk because they stick out of the water and sit at the surface.

> Lightning just hit the ocean.
> Hundreds of millions of volts.
> So why are these fish still swimming?
>
> Because seawater is salty... and salt water carries electricity really well.
>
> So the current doesn't dive down.
> It races outward, across the surface, in every direction.
>
> A few metres down... most of it is already gone.
>
> And salt water carries current better than a fish's body does.
> So it flows around the fish... not through it.
>
> Fish right at the surface, near the strike, can still be killed.
>
> But the real danger isn't under the water.
>
> It's anything sticking out of it.

(~95 words, about 36 s with pauses. The last line cuts straight back to the opening strike, so the video loops.)
