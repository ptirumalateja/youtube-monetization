## Facts and sources

1. **49 Moons in Earth.** Mean radii: Earth 6,371.0 km, Moon 1,737.4 km; (6,371.0 / 1,737.4)^3 = 49.3. Source: NASA Planetary Fact Sheets (nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/).
2. **1,321 Earths in Jupiter.** Jupiter's volumetric mean radius is 69,911 km; (69,911 / 6,371.0)^3 = 1,321. Source: NASA Jupiter fact sheet.
3. **985 Jupiters in the Sun.** Sun radius 695,700 km; (695,700 / 69,911)^3 = 985. Source: NASA Sun fact sheet.
3b. **About 10 billion Suns in Stephenson 2-18.** Radius estimated at ~2,150 solar radii (widely cited figure; estimates vary); 2,150^3 = 9.9 billion, so "about 10 billion". The description states that it is an estimate.
4. **Width lines.** Earth is 3.67 Moons across ("about 4 Moons wide"), Jupiter is 10.97 Earths across ("11x"), the Sun is 9.95 Jupiters across ("10x").
5. **Pinned-comment figure.** (695,700 / 6,371.0)^3 = 1.30 million Earths by volume; NASA lists about 1.3 million.
6. **Packing caveat.** Volume ratios assume no gaps. Equal spheres can fill at most about 74% of a container (Kepler conjecture, proved by Hales 2005), so the heaped balls on screen are fewer than the counter reads. The description says so.
7. **"Stars get much bigger."** True: the Sun is a typical main-sequence G dwarf, while red supergiants such as Betelgeuse are hundreds of times wider. The reel deliberately does not call the Sun "average" or "ordinary", since most stars are smaller red dwarfs.
8. **Visual liberties.** The bowl cut-aways, the ring of light that reveals each next bowl, and the units shrinking into the planet are visual devices; surface maps and proportions are real. The Great Red Spot, bands, Sun granulation and prominences are procedural, not photographs.
