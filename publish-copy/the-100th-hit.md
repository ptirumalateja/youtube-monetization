## Facts and sources

The reel is a parable: the two men, the boulders and the hit counts are story. In the reel the rock breaks on the 100th hit; Riis wrote "the hundred and first blow". The copy quotes him exactly and does not claim that his number is 100.

| Claim in the copy | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Jacob Riis wrote about a stonecutter whose rock split "at the hundred and first blow" | Jacob A. Riis, *The Making of an American* (New York: Macmillan, 1901), p. 253, in the chapter "My Dog Is Avenged". Checked against the Internet Archive scan (`archive.org/details/makingofamerican00riisrich`). | Exact text: "When it didn't seem to help, I would go and look at a stone-cutter hammering away at his rock perhaps a hundred times without as much as a crack showing in it. Yet at the hundred and first blow it would split in two, and I knew it was not that blow that did it, but all that had gone before together." Most quote sites paraphrase it in the present tense ("I go and look..."); keep the original past tense when quoting. |
| Context: when his work "didn't seem to help" | Same page | Riis was campaigning in the newspapers against New York's police-station lodging rooms; he kept writing although nothing seemed to change. Don't call him a stonecutter himself. |
| Jacob Riis was a journalist (1849-1914) | *The Making of an American* (his autobiography); Library of Congress biography | Danish-American newspaper reporter and photographer, author of *How the Other Half Lives* (1890). |

Caveat for replies: the quote is often linked to the San Antonio Spurs, who displayed it in their locker room. That is a later use of the quote, not its source. The reel makes no claim that persistence always pays off; the closing line is "You never know which hit is the last."
