## Caption (IG / FB)
Your brain is built to spot threats fast, so when something is unclear (a late reply, a short tone, a look), it often fills the gap with "they're against me." Psychologists call this hostile attribution bias. The catch is that people react to how you treat them. Go in defensive and they get defensive back, and now the threat you imagined is real. Studies of this bias (Kenneth Dodge's work started in the 1980s) link it to more arguments, more broken friendships and more stress.

The fix is small: before you react, ask "what else could this mean?" Busy, tired, distracted... most of the time it isn't about you.

An old folk tale: a dog walks into a temple hall made of mirrors and sees 1,000 dogs. He growls, they growl. He attacks, they attack. By morning he's dead, and he was the only dog in the room. Another dog walks into the same hall, wags her tail, and 1,000 tails wag back.

Psychologists call the first dog's mistake hostile attribution bias: reading unclear behaviour as an attack. Because people mirror how we treat them, the bias makes itself come true. When a reply is late or a tone is short, ask "what else could this mean?" before you react.
