On the night of August fifteenth, nineteen seventy-seven, Ohio State's Big Ear radio telescope was listening to the sky.

For seventy-two seconds... it heard something.

A signal so strong, and so narrow, that days later, the astronomer reading the printout circled it in red pen... and wrote one word next to it.

"Wow."

It was never heard again.

The search had begun seventeen years earlier. In nineteen sixty, in West Virginia, a young astronomer named Frank Drake pointed a radio dish at two nearby stars, much like our Sun... and listened.

He tuned to the natural frequency of hydrogen, the most common element in the universe. If anyone out there wanted to be heard, the reasoning went, that's where they might call.

For four months, he heard nothing but static.

Ten years before that, the physicist Enrico Fermi had asked a simple question over lunch. If the galaxy is so old, and so vast... where is everybody?

Since then, we've listened with bigger dishes, and better computers. In twenty nineteen, astronomers even caught a signal that seemed to come from our nearest neighbouring star.

It turned out to be our own electronics.

More than sixty years later, the answer is still... silence.

But what if we've been listening for the wrong thing?

Look at Earth from far away. We aren't deliberately sending the universe a message. But we are changing it. Our cities glow at night. Our machines leak radio into space. We've even changed the chemistry of our own air.

So scientists now search for something else.

Technosignatures.

Not a hello. Just evidence that someone, somewhere, built something. Artificial light on a distant planet. A gas in its air that nature doesn't make. Heat, from machines.

Not a voice calling to us. Just the traces a civilisation leaves behind... the way ours does.

And then, there's the question closer to home.

For years, military pilots have reported things in the sky they couldn't identify.

In twenty twenty-one, a U.S. intelligence report reviewed one hundred and forty-four of those reports. It could confidently explain only one. A large, deflating balloon.

Later reviews explained many more. And in twenty twenty-four, a Pentagon office reviewing decades of records... found no evidence of alien technology.

Unexplained doesn't mean alien. It means... unexplained.

So, have we really looked?

In twenty eighteen, astronomers estimated how much of the possible search space all our efforts had covered. Compare it to all of Earth's oceans... and everything we've ever done is about one hot tub of water.

If you scooped a single hot tub out of the ocean, and found no fish... you wouldn't conclude the ocean is empty.

So no. There is no confirmed evidence that aliens are here.

But maybe the silence isn't an answer.

Maybe we just haven't learned how to listen.
